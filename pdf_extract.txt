ABSTRACT Driver drowsiness is a leading contributor to road accidents, accounting for over 100,000
crashes and approximately ∼1000 fatalities each year in the United States alone, as per National Safety
Council (NSC). To mitigate this urgent public-safety risk, we propose a real-time Driver Drowsiness
Detection System that achieves both high accuracy and fast inference on standard hardware. Our key idea is
to combine two complementary deep-learning strategies: 1) a custom Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
paired with a Support V ector Machine (SVM) classifier, and 2) a lightweight transfer-learning model built
upon a pre-trained convolutional backbone. We evaluate these approaches on two datasets: a four-class
Kaggle collection of open/closed eyes and yawn/no-yawn images, and the 37-subject MRL eye dataset. For
the custom CNN+SVM pipeline, we optimized the split ratios, dropout rates, and L2 regularization to achieve
100% training accuracy and 99.7% validation accuracy. For the transfer-learning model, we leveraged an
existing network to accelerate training, achieving 99.4% training accuracy and 99.1% validation accuracy.
Finally, we compare both models using metrics including loss curves, confusion matrices, precision, recall,
and F1-score. Our system demonstrates that real-time, highly accurate drowsiness detection is achievable
without specialized hardware, paving the way for broader deployment in road-safety applications.

I. INTRODUCTION
Driver drowsiness is a leading cause of road accidents, posing
a significant threat to global road safety. According to data
from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration
(NHTSA) [1], thousands of crashes and hundreds of fatalities
occur each year due to drivers falling asleep or losing focus
at the wheel. This alarming trend highlights the urgent
need for practical solutions to detect and prevent drowsy
driving before it leads to catastrophic consequences. Beyond
impaired reaction times and lapses in attention, drowsiness
often manifests subtly—through prolonged eye closures or
yawning—that go unnoticed until it is too late. This urgent
public-safety challenge demands a real-time detection system

II. RELATED WORK
Several studies have been published on drowsiness detection.
Some have used various algorithms, whereas few have
used deep learning methods for driver drowsiness detection.
Drowsiness detection can be modeled using these models.
A mathematical model was developed using statistics and
data analysis [6]. The sleep duration of the participants was
analyzed and modeled. Another method uses fuzzy logic,
in which fuzzy rules and functions are used for analysis.
Machine learning methods include model development using
Support V ector Machines (SVMs) or deep learning models,
such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) [7], [8], [9]. Alshaqaqi focused
on an algorithm for drowsiness detection using face detection
and eye tracking [10], [11]. Eye state and PERCLOS were
measured using the HAAR algorithm and OpenCV for
detection. Razaee mentioned that PERCLOS features the
creation of a real-time intelligent alarm system for eye status
detection that takes video sequences and determines whether
a driver is drowsy. Eyelid movements were used to calculate
human-defined eye features. This provides information on
eyelid movements. The utilization of these artificial features
provides additional information for model development [12],
[13], [14].
Y ong discussed the identification of drowsiness through
emotion recognition, such as smile detection. If no emotion
was detected, the drowsiness condition was detected. This
algorithm is complex and requires time to train [15], [16].
Choi et al. discussed categorization that can be achieved
by developing deep learning models for facial recogni-
tion using driver gaze zones. This model implementation
requires considerable data and works if a high accuracy is
obtained [17], [18]. Trivedi considered only iris status for
drowsiness detection. This study focuses on head analysis,
eye movement, and iris movement. Head movement is not
considered in the development, which could yield better
results [19], [20].
In [21], [22], and [23], the MRL dataset was used to
leverage transfer learning for vehicle safety. An accuracy of
97% was obtained for the drowsiness detection. Assari and
Rahmati used facial expressions to detect drowsiness in their
study. They used a hardware system to detect the face using
infrared light to detect drowsiness [24], [25], [26]. The eye
aspect algorithm and the Raspberry Pi 4 were used to detect

Our survey of existing drowsiness-detection methods
reveals three dominant trends: (1) single-cue approaches
(e.g. PERCLOS or yawning detection) that achieve moderate
accuracy but miss complementary fatigue indicators; (2)
intrusive sensor-based systems (e.g. wearable EEG or ECG)
that offer high reliability under controlled conditions but
suffer from poor user adoption and environmental constraints;
and (3) deep-learning frameworks that deliver state-of-the-
art accuracy yet often require extensive labeled data and
specialized hardware for real-time inference. Few studies
have explored hybrid, multi-cue algorithms that jointly
analyze eye and mouth features, and even fewer have
demonstrated real-time performance on standard webcams
and CPUs. This gap motivates our work: we propose
a non-intrusive, hybrid EAR+MAR detection pipeline,
implemented both as a custom CNN+SVM and a transfer-
learning model, and benchmarked for real-time feasibility on
commodity hardware—thereby addressing both accuracy and
deployment challenges left open in prior literature.

B. CONVOLUTIONAL NEURAL NETWORK (CNN) AND
SUPPORT VECTOR MACHINE (SVM) MODELS FOR
DROWSINESS DETECTION
For our application, we developed two machine-learning
models. In one method, we used deep learning methods, CNN
and SVM, and in another method, we developed a transfer
learning model for drowsiness detection. OpenCV was used
to split the eyes into left and right for eye detection. The
model predicted both open and closed eyes, with a negative
value for closed eyes and positive value for open eyes. A timer
was used to detect the time when eyes were closed. If the
timer is set for a particular threshold value, a drowsy state is
asserted and an alarm is raised to alert the driver.
Two datasets were used to train, test, and validate
the proposed model, respectively. The first dataset was a
four-label dataset containing open-eye, closed-eye, yawning,
and no-yawning images. We used a dataset from Kaggle.
There were two folders: the training and test datasets.
The test and training datasets contain open-eye, closed-
eye, open-mouth, and closed-mouth images. The training
dataset contained 2467 images, and the test dataset contained
433 images. The other dataset is an open-source MRL eye
dataset. It included images of 37 individuals (33 men and four
women). It was not intended for drowsiness but rather only for
pupil detection. This dataset was used to detect drowsiness.
The eyes were divided into closed and open eyes, and grouped
separately.

incorporating parameter sharing and sparse interactions.

2) SUPPORT VECTOR MACHINE (SVM)
The SVM is useful for solving both classification and
regression problems. The support vectors are the nearest
positive and negative points passing through negative and
positive hyperplanes, respectively. SVM kernels convert the
low dimension into a high dimension; therefore, splitting
the positive and negative points is possible. The nonlinear
decision boundary, a key feature of SVM, allows it to handle
complex data distributions. SVM is expressed as f (x) =
beta0 + sumiϵs alphaiK (x, xi), where s is the set of support
vectors. An SVM kernel is a generalization of an inner
product. The kernel can be either a polynomial or radial
kernel.
The SVM model was employed for both binary clas-
sification and multi-class classification tasks. It leverages
kernel functions (such as polynomial or radial basis function
kernels) to handle complex data distributions and nonlinear
decision boundaries. We integrated the SVM model into
the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture to
significantly enhance classification performance. The last
fully connected layer of the CNN was modified to include
an SVM-based output layer. This adaptation allowed the
model to use hinge loss (or squared hinge loss for multi-class
problems) instead of traditional softmax-based loss functions,
significantly improving its ability to distinguish between
classes. The SVM model demonstrated its effectiveness as
a lightweight classifier for drowsiness detection, especially
in scenarios where computational resources are limited.
It provided a baseline for comparing the performance of more
advanced models like CNN and transfer learning, but its
improved performance was awe-inspiring.

C. TRANSFER LEARNING (TL) APPROACH FOR
DROWSINESS DETECTION
In transfer learning, an existing model is used for training,
instead of developing it from scratch. We usually start with
50% to 60% of the already trained models to train our model.
The benefit of using the TL to train our model is that we
do not need to train it from scratch. Simultaneously using
TL to train the model, we must ensure that it is similar to
the classification model being trained. For example, if TL is
trained for a classification model with a similar task, such
as classifying different types of animals, it can be reused for
classifying open and closed eyes. This is because the features
learned by the model for the animal classification task are
likely to be helpful in our eye classification task. Transferring
learning is better than creating a customized model from
scratch.
Different deep learning classifiers, such as MobileNet,
GoogleNet, AlexNet, VGG19, and RestNet101 are popular
for the ImageNet dataset. The ImageNet dataset contains
thousands of images for approximately 1000 classes and 130
GB of data. These classifiers were trained using 1000 classes.
Our study aimed to classify eyes into two groups: open
and closed. It is a binary classifier. The TL uses the last
fully connected layer, which is a key component in transfer
learning. This layer contained approximately 1000 nodes,
each representing a class in the original dataset; however, only
two nodes were required for our binary classification task.
All weights were used using a deep learning architecture.
MobileNet was used to implement this model.

V. LIMITATIONS & FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTION
Despite these advancements, the system is not without
limitations. One significant limitation is its reliance on
facial landmarks, which assumes ideal conditions, such as
proper lighting and unobstructed views of the driver’s face.
Poor lighting, occlusions (e.g., sunglasses or hands covering
the face), or extreme head poses could hinder accurate
detection. Additionally, the system’s performance might
degrade in cases where drivers have unique facial features,
such as heavy eyelids or beards, which could interfere with
calculating EAR and MAR values. Another challenge lies
in the computational requirements of the hybrid EAR-MAR
algorithm, which may pose difficulties for deployment in
resource-constrained environments, such as older vehicles
or embedded systems with limited hardware capabilities.
Furthermore, while effective, the datasets used in this study
may not fully represent the diversity of real-world driving
conditions. Expanding the dataset to include more varied
scenarios and demographic factors will improve the system’s
generalizability.
This research has broader applications beyond road safety.
The methodology can be adapted for monitoring heavy
machinery operators, workers in hazardous environments,
and professionals handling sensitive tasks in industries
such as chemical laboratories and manufacturing plants.
By laying the groundwork for future innovations, this
study highlights the importance of developing smarter, more
effective solutions to combat driver fatigue.

VI. CONCLUSION
Driver’s drowsiness, a significant contributor to road acci-
dents, has been effectively addressed with our practical and
user-friendly Driver Drowsiness Detection System. This sys-
tem, leveraging advanced algorithms and machine learning
techniques, can detect real-time fatigue. It integrates hybrid
EAR and MAR algorithms with state-of-the-art models like
CNN, SVM, and Transfer Learning. Using non-intrusive
facial landmark detection via a webcam eliminates the need
for wearable sensors or additional hardware, making it a
convenient and accessible solution.
This study makes a valuable contribution to the field by
introducing a robust framework that combines traditional
computer vision techniques with modern deep learning
approaches. Transfer learning is particularly noteworthy,
as it reduces computational demands while maintaining high
performance, making the system scalable for a wide range of
real-world applications. Additionally, the hybrid EAR-MAR
algorithm offers a more comprehensive solution for detecting
drowsiness, addressing a wider range of scenarios than
systems that rely solely on individual features like eye
closure or yawning. The novelty of this research is evident
in its holistic approach to driver drowsiness detection.
By combining hybrid EAR-MAR algorithms with state-
of-the-art machine learning models, we have developed a
system that outperforms traditional methods in accuracy
and reliability, instilling optimism and a forward-thinking
mindset in the audience.
The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness
of our approach and instill confidence in the system’s
ability to detect drowsiness reliably. Furthermore, the hybrid
EAR-MAR algorithm proved to be more robust and accurate
than individual EAR or MAR implementations, covering
more scenarios of drowsiness detection.
